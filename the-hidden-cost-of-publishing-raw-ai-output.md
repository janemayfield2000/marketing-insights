<h1 style="font-size: 32px; font-weight: 700; color: #ffffff; margin-bottom: 16px;">The Hidden Cost of Publishing Raw AI Output (And How to Avoid It)</h1>
<img src="https://jpcdn.it/img/small/3d5821eddf39a94baf014d5360de79fc.jpg" alt="The Hidden Cost of Publishing Raw AI Output (And How to Avoid It)" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto; display: block; margin: 20px 0;">

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px; font-size: 16px;">One of the most common mistakes in AI-assisted website work is publishing raw model output without an editorial pass. It reads generic, it often gets facts slightly wrong, and it rarely matches the tone a brand has spent years building. The fix isn't avoiding AI — it's adding a review layer that catches these issues before launch.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px; font-size: 16px;">The second most common mistake is using AI without any section framework. Teams that just ask for "a landing page" tend to get incoherent structure and mismatched messaging. Teams that define their page architecture first — hero, problem framing, solution, proof, CTA, FAQ — and then generate block by block get dramatically more usable results.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px; font-size: 16px;">Speed is another trap. It's tempting to treat AI as a way to skip quality checks entirely, but the real advantage is using that speed to run more iteration cycles, not to cut corners on coherence. A page shipped fast but incoherent will need to be redone anyway, which erases the time saved.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px; font-size: 16px;">Post-launch neglect rounds out the list of common failures. Too many teams treat the initial AI-assisted build as the finished product rather than version one. A monthly light refresh and a quarterly deeper revision keep pages aligned with what's actually converting.</p>
