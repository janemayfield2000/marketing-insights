<h1 style="font-size: 32px; font-weight: 700; color: #ffffff; margin-bottom: 16px;">How Many GPUs Can Your PC Actually Handle?</h1>

<img src="https://jpcdn.it/img/small/416202ebd4b78b3c8af0b562c3d50cd2.png" alt="Multi-GPU PC" style="width: 100%; height: auto; border-radius: 8px; margin-bottom: 24px;">

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Slapping a second or third graphics card into a desktop sounds like an easy way to multiply performance, but the physical slot count on a motherboard is rarely the real ceiling. What actually decides whether a multi-card build works is a combination of available PCIe lane bandwidth, case airflow, power supply headroom, and — most overlooked of all — whether your software can even use more than one GPU efficiently.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Lane allocation is the first wall builders hit. Consumer platforms split bandwidth aggressively once a second card is added, meaning both cards may end up running at reduced lane widths. In bandwidth-heavy workloads, that alone can erase any theoretical gain from the extra hardware.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Physical spacing matters just as much. Modern GPUs are thick, and stacking two of them in adjacent slots often starves the lower card of airflow, leading to thermal throttling that quietly kills clock speeds during long sessions.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Power delivery is another silent killer. A PSU rated just above the combined TDP of two cards might survive a benchmark, but sustained load with transient spikes from CPU, storage, and cooling devices can expose weak margins fast.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Ultimately, the deciding factor is software. If your applications don't split work cleanly across devices, extra GPUs just add heat and cost. For a full breakdown of these constraints and a practical decision framework, this <a href="https://unicornplatform.com/blog/maximize-graphics-card-power-how-many-can-a-pc-have/" style="color: #8ab4f8; text-decoration: none; font-weight: 600;">guide to multi-GPU planning</a> walks through exactly where builds tend to fail and how to avoid it.</p>

<p style="margin-bottom: 18px;">Before buying another card, it's worth benchmarking your actual workload rather than assuming more hardware equals more output — the gap between "technically possible" and "practically worth it" is where most budgets get wasted.</p>
